1 · Energy Stores & Transfers
Energy stores
Eight energy stores
ChemicalEnergy stored in bonds between atoms. Released by combustion, respiration, electrochemical reactions. Examples: fuel, food, batteries.
KineticEnergy of a moving object. Ek = ½mv². Greater mass or speed → more KE.
Gravitational PEEnergy stored due to height above ground. Ep = mgh. Greater height or mass → more GPE.
Thermal (internal)Energy stored in the random motion and vibration of particles. Higher temperature → more thermal energy.
Elastic (strain)Energy stored in stretched or compressed objects (springs, elastic bands, rubber). E = ½ke².
MagneticStored in a magnetic field between magnets.
ElectrostaticStored between charged objects.
NuclearStored in the nucleus of atoms. Released in nuclear reactions (fission, fusion, radioactive decay).
Energy transfers — how energy moves between stores
MechanicallyBy a force causing movement (e.g. pushing, stretching, compressing). Work done = force × distance.
ElectricallyCharge moving in a circuit. E = QV (energy = charge × voltage).
By heatingConduction, convection or radiation — from hotter to cooler region.
By radiationElectromagnetic waves (light, infrared, microwaves, etc.) transfer energy without a medium.

Rockets and satellites — energy store changes

Rocket launchingChemical store (fuel) → Kinetic store + Gravitational PE store + Thermal store (exhaust gases). Thrust must exceed weight.
Rocket in flightAs fuel burns: chemical store decreases; KE and GPE increase. Air resistance converts some KE → thermal.
Satellite in orbitConstant speed → KE constant. Constant height → GPE constant. Gravity provides centripetal force — no energy needed to stay in orbit (ideal).
Falling objectGPE → KE (+ thermal if air resistance present). At terminal velocity: GPE → thermal only.
Conservation of energy
The law of conservation of energy

"Energy cannot be created or destroyed — it can only be transferred between stores or dissipated to the surroundings." The total energy in a closed system remains constant.

Useful energyEnergy transferred to the intended store. e.g. KE in a motor car engine.
Wasted energyEnergy transferred to an unintended store — usually thermal energy of the surroundings (heat loss).
Efficiency% efficiency = (useful output energy ÷ total input energy) × 100. Always ≤ 100%.
Efficiency = (useful output ÷ total input) × 100%
No machine is 100% efficient — energy is always dissipated (lost to surroundings as heat/sound). This does NOT violate conservation of energy — it is just transferred to less useful stores.

2 · Power & Data Handling
Power = rate of energy transfer
P = E ÷ t
PPower (W — watts). 1 W = 1 J/s.
EEnergy transferred (J — joules).
tTime (s — seconds). Convert minutes to seconds first.
E = P × t  |  t = E ÷ P
1 kW = 1000 W. 1 MW = 1 000 000 W. Power is the rate — how quickly energy is transferred, not how much total.

Data handling skills

Presenting dataTables must have headings with quantity AND unit (e.g. "Time / s"). Values to same decimal places. Repeat readings in separate columns.
Anomalous resultA value that does not fit the pattern of other data. Likely a measurement error. Should be circled, excluded from the mean, and noted — do NOT include in mean calculation.
Mean calculationAdd all valid (non-anomalous) values and divide by the number of values. Mean = sum ÷ n. Round to appropriate significant figures.
Repeat readingsTake at least 3 repeats to identify anomalies and calculate a reliable mean. More repeats = more reliable mean.
3 · Pressure
Pressure = Force ÷ Area
P = F ÷ A
PPressure (Pa — pascals or N/m²). 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
FForce (N — newtons). Usually the weight of the object.
AContact area (m² — square metres). Must convert cm² to m².
F = P × A  |  A = F ÷ P
Smaller contact area → HIGHER pressure for same force. This is why sharp objects (needles, knife blades) exert huge pressure.

Measuring pressure — method

  1. Measure the force applied (using a newton meter or calculate weight = mg)
  2. Measure the contact area (length × width in m², or use graph paper to trace the area)
  3. Calculate: P = F ÷ A

Real-life applications

Camels vs humansCamels have wide, flat feet — large area → low pressure on sand (don't sink). Humans have smaller feet → higher pressure.
Skis & snowshoesLarge area → low pressure → don't sink into snow.
ElephantsVery heavy but huge feet → spread force over large area → manageable pressure.
Drawing pins / nailsTiny tip → tiny area → huge pressure from small force.
4 · Specific Heat Capacity (SHC)
SHC — energy per kg per °C
ΔQ = m × c × ΔT
ΔQEnergy transferred (J — joules). Also written as Q or E.
mMass (kg — kilograms). Convert grams to kg first (÷ 1000).
cSpecific heat capacity (J/kg°C or J/kg K). Fixed for each material.
ΔTTemperature change (°C or K). ΔT = T_final − T_initial.
c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT)  |  ΔT = ΔQ ÷ (m × c)

Key SHC values to know

Water4200 J/kg°C — highest common SHC. Heats/cools slowly. Used as coolant.
Aluminium900 J/kg°C. Heats up faster than water for same energy input.
Copper390 J/kg°C. Very low — heats and cools rapidly.
Iron / steel450 J/kg°C.

Advantages of high SHC

  • Water in central heating — absorbs large amounts of heat without large temperature rise, then releases it slowly in radiators
  • Water in car radiators — effective coolant; removes large amounts of heat from the engine
  • Coastal regions — sea has high SHC; moderates local climate (cooler summers, warmer winters)
SHC practical — method to determine c experimentally
Required practical
  1. Measure the mass of the material (m) in kg using a balance
  2. Insulate the sample to minimise energy losses to surroundings
  3. Insert a thermometer and an electrical immersion heater
  4. Connect the heater to a power supply with a voltmeter (V) and ammeter (A)
  5. Record initial temperature (T₁)
  6. Switch on — record time (t), voltage (V) and current (I)
  7. Record final temperature (T₂) after a set time
  8. Calculate energy: Q = V × I × t
  9. Calculate c = Q ÷ (m × ΔT)
Energy from heater: Q = V × I × t
Experimental value of c is always HIGHER than the true value — some energy is lost to heating the container, thermometer and the surrounding air. The block is not perfectly insulated.
To reduce error: use more insulation; use a lid; ensure good thermal contact between heater and block; allow time for temperature to equalise before reading.
Plot temperature (y) against time (x). Gradient = rate of temperature rise. Steeper gradient = higher power input or lower mass/SHC.

VolumeFixedFixedExpands to fill container
Compressible?No — particles touchNo — particles closeYes — large spaces between
DensityHighHigh (slightly lower than solid)Very low
Changes of state — melting, boiling, freezing, condensing
MeltingSolid → Liquid. Particles gain energy to overcome lattice bonds. Begins at melting point. Temperature stays constant during melting.
BoilingLiquid → Gas. Particles gain enough energy to fully escape intermolecular forces. Occurs at boiling point throughout the liquid.
EvaporationLiquid → Gas. Occurs below boiling point at surface only. Faster particles escape. Cools the liquid.
CondensingGas → Liquid. Particles lose energy, slow down, intermolecular forces reform. Releases latent heat.
FreezingLiquid → Solid. Particles lose energy, slow down enough to form lattice structure.
SublimationSolid → Gas directly (e.g. dry ice/CO₂). No liquid stage.
During any change of state, temperature remains constant even though energy is still being supplied. Energy is used to break (or form) intermolecular bonds — not to increase particle kinetic energy. This is called latent heat.
Using particle model to explain observations
Pressure in gasesGas particles collide with container walls → exert force per unit area = pressure. More collisions or harder collisions → higher pressure.
Gases are compressibleLarge spaces between gas particles — can be pushed closer together. Solids/liquids already close-packed.
Diffusion in gasesGas particles move rapidly and randomly → spread out from high to low concentration. Faster at higher temperature.
Thermal expansionHeating → particles vibrate more → take up more space → material expands. Solids expand least; gases expand most.
Why solids hold shapeStrong intermolecular forces hold particles in fixed lattice positions — particles can only vibrate, not move past each other.
DissolvingSolute particles spread between solvent particles — space between liquid particles allows this.
6 · Heating Curves & Graphs
Temperature vs time graph — key features

Sloped sections (temperature rising)

Solid heatingEnergy increases particle vibration — KE increases — temperature rises.
Liquid heatingEnergy increases random motion of liquid particles — KE increases — temperature rises.
Gas heatingEnergy increases speed of gas particles — KE increases — temperature rises.

Flat sections (temperature constant)

At melting pointEnergy breaks intermolecular bonds in lattice — solid → liquid. Temperature stays constant. This energy is called LATENT HEAT OF FUSION.
At boiling pointEnergy overcomes all intermolecular forces — liquid → gas. Temperature stays constant. This is LATENT HEAT OF VAPORISATION.
On a cooling curve, the flat sections appear at the same temperatures but energy is released (not absorbed) as bonds reform.
7 · Thermal Energy Transfer
Conduction

Transfer of thermal energy through a solid (or between solids in contact) by particle vibration. Hot particles vibrate more → collide with neighbours → pass on energy.

Metals conduct very well — free electrons carry thermal energy rapidly through the structure (in addition to particle vibration).

Good conductorsMetals (copper, aluminium, iron). Used in: saucepans, radiators, heat sinks, soldering irons.
Poor conductorsWood, plastic, glass, air, wool. Used as insulators: handles, building insulation, double glazing.
Convection

Transfer of thermal energy through fluids (liquids and gases) by the bulk movement of the fluid itself.

Mechanism: Fluid heats up → expands → becomes less dense → rises. Cooler, denser fluid flows in to replace it → creates a convection current.

ExamplesHeating a room (hot air rises from radiator, circulates). Boiling water. Sea breezes. Weather systems.
Cannot occur inSolids — particles cannot flow. Only works in fluids (liquids and gases).
Radiation (Infrared)

Transfer of thermal energy by infrared electromagnetic waves. Does NOT require a medium — can travel through a vacuum (e.g. heat from the Sun).

Matt black surfaceBest absorber AND best emitter of infrared radiation. Maximum emission and absorption.
Shiny white surfaceBest reflector. Worst absorber and worst emitter. Minimum emission and absorption.
All objectsEmit and absorb infrared. Hotter objects emit more radiation at shorter wavelengths.

Real-life thermal transfer applications

Snow on roofsSnow is white/light-coloured — poor emitter of IR. Stays on roof longer because less heat is radiated away. Also insulates: trapped air is a poor conductor.
Metal surfacesShiny metal (e.g. aluminium foil, emergency blanket) reflects IR → reduces heat loss by radiation.
Vacuum flaskDouble-walled glass with vacuum between walls (no conduction/convection). Silvered walls (reduce radiation). Stopper reduces convection at top.
Solar panelsMatt black surface — maximises absorption of solar radiation.
Loft insulationFibreglass traps air — air is poor conductor. Reduces conduction heat loss through roof.
